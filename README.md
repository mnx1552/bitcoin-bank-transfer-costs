## What a bank-transfer purchase costs, line by line

A bank-transfer order is usually three charges stacked on top of each other: what your bank takes to send the money, what the platform takes as commission, and the Bitcoin network fee for the outgoing transaction. Paybis publishes all three for its SEPA route, which makes it a useful reference point: a fixed **€0.10** bank fee, a **1%** platform commission, and the miner fee that goes to the network.

| Rail | What you pay | Where the number comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SEPA (euro) | €0.10 bank fee + 1% commission + miner fee | Paybis' published SEPA breakdown |
| Bank transfer, general | 0.5%–1% of the order | Banxa's stated range, about a quarter of card pricing |
| Card | Roughly 3%–5%, derived from £300–£500 per £10,000 | Banxa's card-versus-transfer comparison |
| SWIFT | £15–£30 intermediary bank fee, or 3%–4% bank processing fee | Banxa (£15–£30) and Binance's P2P guide (3%–4%) |

The card gap is where the decision gets made. On a £10,000 order, card fees run £300–£500 and bank transfer runs £50–£100. Card pricing buys you an instant price lock and a chargeback window; the transfer route gives up both.

SWIFT is reported two different ways because it has two different cost components. Banxa quotes intermediary bank fees of £15–£30 per transfer; Binance's guide describes banks charging about 3%–4% to process a SWIFT payment. Both can apply at once, since the sending bank's commission and the intermediary bank's deduction are separate line items. Run the arithmetic on a small order and SWIFT stops making sense: on £1,000, a £15–£30 hit is already 1.5%–3%, which is most or all of what the transfer route saves you over a card.

## How long each rail takes, and what the price does while you wait

Settlement speed depends on your bank's rail, not on the exchange. No platform can hurry a transfer along once it's in the banking system; processing starts when the funds arrive and clear.

| Rail | Typical settlement | Price exposure while it clears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instant SEPA | Under 1 minute, if your bank supports it | Minimal |
| Standard SEPA credit transfer | 1–2 business days | A weekend in the middle can move the price |
| UK Faster Payments | Minutes, sometimes under an hour | Low |
| US ACH | 1–3 business days | Highest of the domestic rails |
| SWIFT | 1–5 business days | Longest window, plus intermediary deductions |
| In-country domestic transfer | Usually within 24 hours | Depends on your bank's cut-off times |

Bitcoin doesn't wait for your bank. In the seven days to 14 November 2026, BTC fell about 12%, and it was down 3% in the 24 hours before that snapshot. A two-day ACH transfer can land you at a noticeably different price than the one you saw at checkout, in either direction. That's not a reason to avoid bank transfers; it's a reason to size the order and pick the rail with the wait in mind.

## Three things that get bank-transfer orders delayed or rejected

### 1. The name on the account doesn't match

Your bank account name has to match your exchange account name exactly. Not a spouse's account. Not a joint account held under a different name. Not a business account, unless the exchange account was verified under that business name. Banxa calls name mismatches the single most common reason bank-transfer purchases get delayed or rejected, and it isn't a policy a support agent can waive: it's an anti-money-laundering requirement.

### 2. The reference code is wrong or missing

Every transfer you make to an exchange carries a unique reference that ties the payment to your order. Leave it blank, retype it from memory instead of pasting, or send from an unverified account, and the money arrives with no owner attached. It doesn't disappear. It does take a support ticket and several days to match a misattributed payment back to your order, during which the price keeps moving.

### 3. The price locks at the wrong moment

A card purchase locks the price when you click buy. A bank transfer usually locks it when the platform receives and clears your funds. Order on a Friday, money lands Tuesday, you get Tuesday's price. Some platforms offer a price-lock window after order creation, but the terms differ and the window is usually short. Read the one in front of you before you send.

One more property worth internalising: settled bank transfers are final. There's no chargeback and no reversal window, which is precisely why the rail is cheap, since the platform carries zero fraud risk on your payment. It also means a mistyped account number or a doubled amount is your problem to solve with your bank, not the exchange's.

## FAQ

**How long does buying bitcoin with a bank transfer take?**
Under a minute on Instant SEPA if your bank supports it, 1–2 business days on standard SEPA, minutes on UK Faster Payments, 1–3 business days on US ACH, and 1–5 business days on SWIFT. The clock starts when your bank sends, and the exchange only begins processing once the funds arrive and clear.

**How much cheaper is it than paying by card?**
On a £10,000 order, roughly £200–£400 cheaper. Bank transfer runs 0.5%–1%, while card fees on that amount come to £300–£500. The gap narrows on small orders and widens on large ones, which is why the rail choice matters most above a few thousand.

**Can I pay from a joint account or a family member's account?**
No. The sending account name has to match your verified exchange account name. Third-party payments get flagged and held under anti-money-laundering rules, and there's no appeals process that gets around the name check.

**What happens if the price drops while my transfer is in transit?**
You get the price at the moment the platform receives and clears your funds, not the price you saw when you started the order. Some platforms offer a limited price-lock window after order creation; check the terms before you rely on one.

**Can I buy without completing verification?**
Most legitimate exchanges require at least basic verification for bank-account purchases, because the bank has to match the account holder's name against your exchange profile. That check typically takes a few minutes and only happens once. Some US platforms allow purchases up to $1,000 with just an email and phone number, but that's their own product rule, not an industry standard, and it doesn't apply to bank-transfer funding.

## Which rail to use, by where you bank

In the SEPA zone with a bank that supports Instant SEPA, use it: under a minute, €0.10 plus commission, no meaningful price exposure. In the UK, Faster Payments beats everything on speed and adds nothing to the cost. In the US, ACH at 1–3 days is the sensible default, and wiring £15–£30 through SWIFT to buy a few hundred pounds of bitcoin is a waste of the fee. If your country has no local rail into the exchange, P2P over a domestic transfer usually beats an international wire on both cost and speed.

Two things to confirm before you hit send: the beneficiary name matches your verified account name character for character, and the reference code is pasted rather than retyped. Those two fields, not the fee table, decide whether your order clears today or sits in a support queue.
